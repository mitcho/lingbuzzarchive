Yat as a Marker of PIE Vowel Lability: Evidence from Old Church Slavonic and Early Slavic Loanwords
Marko Jevremović
April 2026
 

The Old Church Slavonic letter yat (Ѣ, ѣ) represents Proto-Slavic *ě, a vowel unique among Slavic phonemes for producing four distinct modern reflexes: Ekavian /e/, Ikavian /i/, Ijekavian /ije/, and Bulgarian /ja/. We demonstrate that this four-way divergence is not a Slavic-internal accident but a continuation of a deeper pattern: yat systematically marks Proto-Indo-European (PIE) phonological positions where the vowel nucleus was complex—either a laryngeal sequence (*eh1) or a diphthong (*ey, *oy, *ay). We construct a 74-word corpus of yat-bearing OCS words with established PIE etymologies and quantify cross-IE vowel divergence at each yat position using a formal Lability Score (LS). Of 67 testable entries, 91% show at least moderate cross-IE vowel lability (mean LS = 3.4), compared with 2.64 for a control set of 53 non-yat *e words (p<0.001). An independent loanword test examining 45 early borrowings into Proto-Slavic confirms that *ai diphthongs and long *ē in donor languages were mapped to yat with 98% accuracy, while other vowel types were never assigned yat (Fisher’s exact test, p<0.0001). A test for correlation between PIE source group and dialect-internal yat reflex exceptions yields a negative result, indicating that the two PIE sources had fully merged before intra-Slavic divergence began. We argue that yat functions as a graphemic marker of inherited PIE vowel instability—a “written laryngeal” that captures the phonological class where IE branches systematically disagree on vowel quality.
