Overgeneralization of (Anti)causativization in L2 English Acquisition: Learners’ Acceptability Judgments of Overanticausativized Transitive/Causative Verbs
Yu Tazaki
February 2026
 

This thesis investigates the overgeneralization of the causative alternation in second language (L2) English, with particular focus on over(anti)causativization errors, whereby non-alternating transitive or causative verbs are accepted in ungrammatical (anti)causative constructions. While previous research has extensively documented overcausativization errors involving unaccusative verbs (e.g., *The magician disappeared the rabbit), comparatively little attention has been paid to learners’ acceptance of non-target anticausative uses such as *The picture painted. Building on the manner/result distinction proposed in lexical semantic theory, this study advances a manner/result overgeneralization hypothesis, according to which L2 learners overextend the generalization that only result verbs, which lexicalize the result state of the event, can participate in the causative alternation. Once learners acquire this generalization, they may incorrectly apply it to non-alternating result verbs (destroy, kill), while manner verbs are predicted to resist such overgeneralization. This proposal makes predictions that differ from existing accounts based on L1 lexical transfer or the assumption that learners uniformly assign complex event structures to all verbs. To test this hypothesis, a context-based 7-point acceptability judgment task was administered to 120 Japanese-speaking learners of English across four proficiency levels, as well as a native English control group. The experiment employed a 2 × 2 design crossing Verb Type (manner vs. result verbs) and Structure (transitive/causative vs. anticausative). All target verbs were non-alternating in both English and Japanese, allowing the effects of lexical semantics to be examined independently of L1 transfer. The judgment data were z-score transformed and analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. The results revealed that learners at the intermediate proficiency level rated anticausativized result verbs significantly higher than anticausativized manner verbs, while no such asymmetry was observed in lower or higher proficiency groups. This pattern supports the manner/result overgeneralization hypothesis and suggests that intermediate learners possess knowledge of the semantic generalization governing the causative alternation but overapply it to non-alternating result verbs. The findings indicate that non-target (anti)causativization in L2 English reflects a developmental stage in which learners generalize lexical-semantic principles, rather than mere L1 transfer or undifferentiated event-structure representations.
