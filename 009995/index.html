The loud silence of echo
Maria Esipova
May 2026
 

'Echo questions', or 'echos' for short, ask the addressee to clarify the utterance they just produced by repeating (some part of) it. We can distinguish between polar echos (asking if their prejacent matches the antecedent utterance) and wh-echos (asking a wh-question about some part of the antecedent utterance). We can also have echos to antecedent utterances of different types. Thus, (1)-(4) illustrate polar and wh-echos in English to assertions, polar questions, wh-questions, and imperative directives.

(1) a. A: I gave you a book.
B: {You gave me a book? / You gave me what? / What did you give me?}
(2) A: Did you give me a book?
B: {Did I give you a book? / Did I give you what?}
(3) A: What did you give me?
B: {What did I give you? / What did I what?}
(4) A: Give me a book.
B: {Give you a book? / Give you what?}

In English, echos tend to use non-neutral forms (e.g., rising declaratives and imperatives, wh-in-situ questions), but these forms typically still have non-echo uses, and in general, the internal structure of English echos is not always clear from their form. While it is commonly assumed that prosody can distinguish echo vs. non-echo questions, Biezma et al. (2021) claim that neither word order, nor prosody reliably distinguish echo vs. non-echo interpretations of wh-questions in English and conclude that the difference between echo and non-echo questions (of any type) is not their compositional semantic representation, but "their position in the discourse". In other languages, people have observed form differences between echo and non-echo questions that ostensibly necessitate distinct compositional representations. For instance, Sudo (2007) shows that Japanese echo vs. non-echo questions use different particles and proposes that the 'tte' particle, which only occurs in echo questions, realizes a special ECHO complementizer.

In this paper, I look at morphosyntactic and prosodic properties of different types of echos in Russian. I show that while there doesn't seem to be a systematic overt way to realize any kind of ECHO operator in Russian (segmental or suprasegmental), we have evidence that echos in Russian systematically involve embedding under a silent ECHO predicate, which is a speech report predicate anaphoric to the antecedent utterance.
