The syntax of verb-object phrases in Chinese: constraints and reanalysis
Waltraud Paul
September 2026
 

This revised version of my doctoral dissertation was published nearly four decades ago in 1988 and available on my website since 2010 (https://crlao.cnrs.fr/membres/waltraud-paul/). The reason for posting it now on lingbuzz with its much larger audience is the renewed interest by neurolinguists in the data set extensively discussed in my thesis. Here’s the background. Chinese has verb-object phrases (VOP) whose meaning can be compositional or non-compositional: bō ‘remove’ pí ‘skin’ = ‘peel’, kāi ‘open’ dāo ‘knife’ = ‘operate (on)’. Given their phrasal status, the verb can bear aspectual suffixes and the object can be modified; only the latter shows some constraints in the case of idiomatic VOPs. The patient of the VOP as a whole (i.e. the fruit to be peeled, the person to be operated on) must not follow the VOP, due to the Phrase Structure Condition, a general principle of Chinese syntax (cf. C.-T. James Huang 1982, ch. 2). Instead, it can occur in preverbal position, preceded by bǎ (still misanalysed as a preposition in Paul 1988), gěi ‘to, for’ or gēn ‘with’. The particular form for encoding the patient needs to be specified in the lexicon, both for compositional and non-compositional VOPs. Against this background, the thesis uses a fine-grained analysis of VOPs as a red thread to explore different core domains of Chinese syntax. Notwithstanding the myriad of syntactic arguments in favour of the phrasal status of VOPs, they are still often considered verbs, i.e. words (V°), which for mysterious reasons can be “separated” (e.g. by an aspectual suffix on the verb: V-Asp O), hence the label "separable verbs". These contrast with so-called "inseparable verbs", i.e. bona fide compound verbs (V°) such as chū ‘produce’- bǎn ‘printing.plate’ = ‘publish’ ([chū-(*Asp) bǎn] -Asp) . The main reason for the incorrect analysis as words (V°) seems to be the often observed non-compositionality of meaning and the (mis)assumption of an isomorphism between syntax and semantics. Neurolinguistic research now (mis) takes these so-called “separable verbs” (i.e. VO-phrases) as representative of the morphological domain and performs different experiments, inter alia with the aim to challenge the distinctness between morphology and syntax via the obtained neural responses (cf. as the most recent example Yu et al 2026 and similar studies cited there: https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.70220). Unsurprisingly, the results are often not very conclusive and quite a lot of speculations are offered in terms of explanation. However, no-one goes back to their initial experimental design and realizes that their purported words are in fact phrases.
