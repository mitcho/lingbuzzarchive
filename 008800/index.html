The sounds of Mandarin: Learning to speak a national language (book review)
Norbert Francis
February 2025
 

How do languages become national by motivating diverse populations to converge on a target of learning? The study by Professor Chen begins with the period in Chinese history following the 1911 Revolution that marked the beginning of a great social and linguistic turning point: the New Culture Movement, precursor of the May Fourth Movement of 1919. Hu Shi, writing the prescient essay “Constructive literary revolution – A literature of national speech” in a 1918 number of New Youth, called for the wide-ranging reform of language and writing: “Speak in the language of the time in which you live” – baihua 白話 (clear speech, plain speech). The reference was to what is often called the “vernacular.” One might ask in hindsight, for the sake of clarity, which language was this “vernacular,” or in other words: to which language, specifically, did this “vernacular” belong? As the book outlines in detail, the pathway to fully realizing the reform across the provinces would turn out to be steep and protracted.
