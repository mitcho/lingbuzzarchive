Boundary tones as sandhi triggers in Xiamen-Taiwanese
Andrew Ira Nevins, Nicholas Rolle
July 2025
 

In this chapter, we revisit tone sandhi in the Xiamen-Taiwanese cluster of Southern Min. Xiamen-Taiwanese has a seven-way tone contrast where each tone has two forms, one found in phrase-final position (which includes words in isolation) and one in non-final position. Traditionally, the form in final position is taken as the basic form (i.e. underlying), from which the non-final form is derived through its famous tone circle (24→33→21→52→44→33). This set of alternations is frequently characterized as unnatural and underivable in formal phonology, and is instead said to require a special para-phonological process of ‘paradigmatic replacement’. We argue against this traditional analysis, and instead interpret the non-final forms as the basic forms from which final forms are derived. In our analysis, we decompose the seven tones into tonal features [H] and [L] and register features [h] and [l], rendering the phonological representation distinct from a tone’s surface pitch. To derive the final forms of words, we posit that each phonological phrase ends in a polar register boundary tone (abbreviated (r)), which surfaces with the opposite value of the register of its base. By positing this one single phonological unit (r), we can account for all tonal alternations. In total, under our analysis the sandhi system is far from arbitrary, underivable, or even peculiar to Xiamen-Taiwanese: it is a well-behaved tonal system involving pre-established notions of tonal features, boundary tones, polarity, and markedness repairs (e.g. spreading and delinking).
