Contrast via anti-additivity: The cases of Chinese "bìng bù" (and Korean "talum anila")
Linmin Zhang
August 2025
 

Contrastive particles typically mark contrast (e.g., signaling a twist between clauses) or serve a corrective function, countering an expectation. In this paper, I focus on the use of Chinese "bìng bù" and extend the discussion to Korean "talum anila" (or "talun-key anila"). I show that both Chinese "bìng bù" and Korean "talum anila" contain an additive particle (bìng, talum) and a negation operator (bù, anila). I propose anti-additivity (regarding meaning strengthening) as a mechanism of establishing contrast. The meaning of ‘(q,) bìng bù p’ can be paraphrased as ‘(q, but) contrary to what someone might think, ¬p’. The speaker of ‘(q,) bìng bù p’ (i) acknowledges p (i.e., the prejacent of "bìng bù") as a salient implicature (or other salient or likely information) given the current common ground (CG, q included), and (ii) explicitly rejects p as part of the CG. Similarly, ‘talum anila, q’ assumes stronger alternatives beyond what is uttered (here q) and negates those alternatives. In using "bìng bù" and "talum anila", the speaker makes explicit ‘q ∧ ¬(q ∧ p)’ (q is information already included in the CG). Negation functions to block the strengthening of q into q ∧ p, thereby enforcing a non-additive interpretation of q. The speaker operates this constraint on meaning strengthening at the sentence level as CG management, disrupting interlocutors’ default expectations in interpreting q and developing the CG, thus producing a contrastive effect.
