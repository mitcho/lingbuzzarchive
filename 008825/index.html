English (stative) passive participles: Category, argument introduction, and interpretation
Alison Biggs, David Embick
February 2025
 

We develop a syntactic analysis of stative passive participles in English: cf. "the flattened box...", "The box is flattened". One focus is on new generalizations about stative passive interpretation: in predicative position the participle has a stative interpretation, while in attributive position it has both a stative and a purely eventive interpretation; this leads to the question of why these meanings are distributionally conditioned. A second focus concerns the stative passive’s eventive interpretation. This raises the question of how stative passive participles relate to the eventive passive participle, seen in "The box was flattened by Susan". The two participles share a non-verbal distribution; they are also invariably identical in form in English. Yet they differ as well: the eventive passive has only an eventive interpretation, while the stative participle cannot be eventive in predicative syntax. We explain the similarities between the two passives by positing a head α in both. This head defines a non-verbal syntactic distribution, and accounts for the shared form. For the differences, what α attaches to is different in the two participles: in the eventive passive, the complement of α is a phrasal VoiceP; in the stative passive, it is a small (non-phrasal) verbalized structure (i.e. a complex head). We account for the syntactically-sensitive interpretations of the stative passive in connection with argument introduction: when there is an argument introduced by the head i∗, the interpretation of α is stative; otherwise the interpretation is eventive. This (contextual) allosemy of α provides an explanation for the attributive/predicative asymmetry by admitting an eventive interpretation only when the participle does not introduce its own argument, as is possible in attributive syntax. Taken as a whole, this analysis requires an abstract view of syntactic categories, in which a particular category like α does not necessarily correlate with a single type of argument introduction, or a single interpretation (stative or eventive). Our discussion examines the implications of this view, and questions concerning the relation between α and the category ‘adjective’, the nature of allosemy, and the prospects for exploring these matters cross-linguistically.
