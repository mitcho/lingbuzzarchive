Don't be so Negative: Towards Negation-Free Syntax and Semantics
Peter Ludlow
June 2026
 

Proof theorists in logic and mathematics have long been suspicious of the classical (Boolean) approach to negation. This suspicion began with work by Brouwer and Griss in the foundations of mathematics and extended to Heyting's proof theory for logic. Griss, in particular, inveighed against using negation in any form in proof theory. Setting aside questions about proof theory in mathematics and logic, what can we say about classical negation in a proof-theoretic semantics for natural language? Can we dispense with it? I argue that we can and, based on the evidence, we should. This suggests a corollary hypothesis: There is no genuine classical negation in the syntax of natural language. The words that we call "negations" ('no', 'not', etc.) are really just voiced indicators of something else altogether: abstract polarity markers-primitive ↓ annotations on formulas that signal downward entailment environments and direct our inferential practice. Everything that linguists need to explain can be accomplished with those minimal resources and without classical negation.
