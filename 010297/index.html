Against trivializing fieldwork; or, why Tobati is perhaps not OSV.
Nikolaus P. Himmelmann
September 2026
 

After a long period of neglect, the discipline has rediscovered the importance of fieldwork in the last few decades and significantly increased fieldworking activities. The present contribution is concerned with the fact that the renewed interest in fieldwork and data from fieldwork has largely failed to recognize that not all fieldwork is created equal. There is an obvious difference between a chapter on the basic case system of language X that is based on 30 years of continued and extensive work with a particular community, and a brief statement that is based on the first stay in a community lasting only a few hours or days. Ignoring this difference risks trivializing fieldwork whenever one is confronted with statements based on fieldwork data. Things become quite problematic when publications based on fieldwork make claims with regard to highly conspicuous and marked phenomena, such as the claim that a language’s dominant word order is OSV (making it one of only four such languages currently listed in the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS)), or that the language in question has very long, morphologically complex words. The current contribution provides short case studies on two languages for which exactly these claims have been made: Oceanic Tobati (OSV word order) and West Bomberai Iha (‘exuberantly inflected verbs’). In both instances, the publications give no indication whatsoever of the data basis for their claims.
