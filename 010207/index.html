Plural Predication and Scope
Jad Wehbe
August 2026
 

A central question in formal semantics concerns the division of labor between lexical semantics and the compositional system. Given a particular inference pattern, we can ask whether it is encoded in the lexicon or whether it arises from a particular syntactic interaction between different compositional ingredients. This question is especially important in the domain of plural predication, where there are competing lexical and syntactic accounts for a variety of phenomena. This dissertation argues, via a detailed investigation of cumulative inferences, reciprocal alternations and the interpretation of `together', for a syntactic approach according to which these core inferences in plural predication result from different scope interactions between a limited set of syntactic operator. The dissertation therefore argues for a more impoverished lexicon and a richer syntax in the domain of plural predication. The scope-based account faces an immediate explanatory challenge: while lexical analyses appeal to lexical generalizations to capture the distribution of readings in each construction, the scope-based account appears to overgenerate unattested readings. I show that these lexical stipulations can be replaced by independently motivated grammatical constraints that cut across different constructions. More specifically, I propose that two such constraints play a key role in plural predication: (i) a generalized economy constraint, inspired by Fox's (2000) scope economy, which rules out LFs when they can be proven to be equivalent to simpler LFs, and (ii) a constraint that requires implicit arguments to take lowest scope (Fodor and Fodor, 1980). The result is a framework in which the different ingredients of plural predication are in principle free to take scope in different positions, as long as they don't violate these independently needed constraints. Finally, the scope-based account has some broader architectural consequences. The first consequence concerns the nature of economy constraints in the grammar. I show that in order to predict the desired distribution of readings, the generalized economy constraint has to be evaluated relative to an encapsulated system which only has access to some of the information that is needed to compute truth-conditions. My account therefore provides a new domain where we see evidence for a modular deductive system (Fox, 2000; Gayewski, 2002; Fox and Hackl, 2006; Chierchia, 2013, a.o.). The second consequence concerns the division of labor between the lexicon and the syntax. I suggest that the impoverished role of the lexicon in plural predication might reflect broader constraints on what kinds of meanings can be encoded in a single lexical item.
