Emotion-Driven Hypothesis for the Origins of Language. From Cross-Species Research to Neuroscience: Exploring the Evolutionary Pathways of Language
Chun-Hao Chan
June 2025
 

This paper proposes an emotion-driven hypothesis for the origins of language, integrating insights from evolutionary biology, neurocognition, and cross-species behavioral analysis. It argues that language did not emerge solely as a tool for information transmission or social bonding, but as a symbolic scaffolding for resolving increasingly complex emotional-cognitive conflicts in early hominins. Drawing on comparative evidence from eusocial species and human brain developmental neuroscience, the study introduces two core models: (1) the Emotion–Intention–Reasoning Chain, which maps the internal logic of emotionally motivated communication, and (2) the Ec × Cn × Ns tri-axis framework, which describes how ecological, cognitive, and social factors jointly pressure symbolic evolution. The hypothesis further links language development to recursive narrative structures and affective role modeling, suggesting that the emergence of syntax reflects a deeper neuro-emotional need for nested mental state simulation. By reframing language as a byproduct of emotional tension resolution and symbolic compensation, this work opens a new interdisciplinary avenue connecting linguistics, evolutionary theory, and affective cognition. While recent works have emphasized the co-evolutionary interplay between language and emotions, particularly their mutual shaping across human development and culture (e.g., Jablonka et al., 2022), this study steps further back in the evolutionary timeline to ask a more fundamental question: what emotional and cognitive pressures triggered the very emergence of language in the first place? This paper does not merely explore how language and emotion evolved together—it asks why language had to begin at all.
