Children's knowledge of negative free choice inferences
Lyn Tieu, Paul Marty, Jacopo Romoli
April 2026
 

Sentences of the form ♢(A∨B), such as "Mia is allowed to buy the apple or the banana", give rise to a free choice inference (FCI), namely the inference that each disjunct is individually permitted, i.e. ♢A∧♢B. Previous studies have shown that children as young as five years of age compute such inferences. In this paper, we investigate negative free choice inferences (NFCIs). Sentences of the form ¬□(A∧B), such as "Mia doesn’t have to buy both the apple and the banana", give rise to the inference that each of the conjuncts is not required, i.e. ¬□A∧¬□B. Adults reportedly derive such inferences less robustly than positive FCIs, a result that has been taken to support hybrid approaches on which positive FCIs are derived by a non-implicature mechanism, while NFCs are genuine implicatures. Given children’s documented difficulties with scalar implicatures, this approach predicts that they will likewise have difficulty deriving NFCs. We report two truth-value judgment experiments conducted with English-speaking adults and 5- to 7-year-old children, comparing positive and negative FCIs with positive and negative scalar implicatures. Experiment 1 tested modal statements; Experiment 2 extended the same contrasts to the nominal domain. In both experiments, children were largely adult-like with respect to positive FCIs. However, their behavior with NFCs did not conform to the predictions of the hybrid approach. Although our findings replicate the polarity asymmetry previously observed in adults, we argue that the developmental data ultimately challenge the hybrid account of FCIs.
