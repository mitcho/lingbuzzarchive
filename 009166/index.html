Negation in Mandarin Chinese and the specialness of states
Waltraud Paul, Shanshan Yan
November 2025
 

Despite numerous studies of negation in Mandarin Chinese over the last 50 years and a wealth of important observations, there has been no consensus so far on how to capture in a systematic way the differences between bù ‘not’ and méi (yǒu) ‘not have’. The present article argues that the same dichotomy “state vs non-state predicates” that is crucial for finiteness in Mandarin Chinese (cf. Ramchand 2020, to appear; Paul & Ramchand 2023) also provides the key to an encompassing analysis of these two negation markers. This is in line with the well-known relation established for languages with overt tense morphology between negation and tense as the locus of finiteness (cf. Zanuttini 1997). Bù ‘not’ realizes the head of the functional category Negation Phrase. It negates states (including habituals) in the present and past tense. When negating non-states, there is a covert future/volitional modal ‘will/would not; do/did not want to’. Méi (yǒu) ‘not have’ is an auxiliary. It negates non-states in the past as well as “derived states” in the past and present, i.e. predicates marked with the progressive aspect zài (‘zài V’) or the durative aspect -zhe (‘V-zhe’). Crucially, neither bù not’ nor méi (yǒu) ‘not have’ can be associated with a fixed temporal value.
