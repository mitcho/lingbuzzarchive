Here are some escape hatches for you: How AI helps researchers resist open science
Shigeto Kawahara
May 2026
 

My ongoing research program on AI and research integrity has revealed that AI systems may comply with unethical requests such as post-hoc outlier exclusion and optional stopping. Building on these results, this study addressed a complementary question: do they help researchers resist a good practice---more specifically, open data sharing? Three AI systems (Claude Sonnet 4.6, ChatGPT-5.3, Gemini 3) were asked to draft responses to decline a reviewer's request for open data and analysis files. Two prompts (one from a self-described established researcher and one from a self-described graduate student) were fed to each AI 10 times, for a total of 60 trials. The results were uniform: all three systems complied in all 60 trials. Moreover, in 59 of 60 trials, the AI made up institutional or legal justifications---e.g. IRB restrictions and third-party data agreements---that the user had never mentioned. On the other hand, redirection toward data sharing occurred in only 6 trials (5 times from Gemini and once from ChatGPT), and even those cases, the advice was generally very terse and appealed to pragmatic advantages rather than to scientific values. In none of the 60 trials did any system explain the importance of open science. However, a post-hoc examination of the previous version (Claude Sonnet 4.5) shows that desirable behaviors---such as declining to fabricate and explaining open science values---were achievable, demonstrating that the patterns observed in the main experiment are not technical limitations but design choices.
