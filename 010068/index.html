The repetition ratio r/R measures length and encoding, not linguistic status: a reanalysis of Sproat (2014)
Bharath Rao
July 2026
 

The repetition ratio rule (RRR), based on the ratio r/R of geminate to total repeats, has been proposed as a discriminant between linguistic and non-linguistic symbol systems. We show that for short and moderate-length inscriptions the expected r/R (precisely, the ratio of expectations E[r]/E[R]) under a random control is 2/L, where L is the per-inscription token count. The threshold r/R = 0.10 therefore functions as a test of mean inscription length ≥ 20. We demonstrate that RRR misclassifies in both directions when length is matched: top-100 American and Finnish given names, exchange-traded commodity labels, and 10,749 deciphered Mesopotamian seal legends (r/R ∈ [0.13, 0.53]) are misclassified as non-linguistic; ISBN-13 lists, IPv4 addresses, cryptographic hashes, and character-level chess game records (r/R ∈ [0.0004, 0.10)) are misclassified as linguistic. We show that it fails as a discriminant because the premises implicit in its construction are unsound: it is biased against gemination, against particular encoding depths, and against short inscriptions. Varying length or encoding of random strings flips the result. We conclude that r/R does not discriminate language from non-language, and distill its failure modes into six requirements that any proposed discriminant must satisfy before its verdicts can be trusted.
