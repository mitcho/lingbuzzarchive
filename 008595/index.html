On hopeful monsters and the origin of language and humanity
Svetlana T.davidova
November 2024
 

For most scholars evolution by Darwinian principles of gradual accumulation of minor adaptive improvements is accepted as the standard explanation for the observable diversity of life forms. That said, the explanation of the notable gap between human cognitive and linguistic abilities and those of our evolutionary predecessors by evoking Darwinian gradualism has been unconvincing to some. An alternative explanation involving a founder effect, initiated by a hopeful monster, produced by a single genetic accident with transforming effects has been proposed. This saltationist argument has been particularly influential among students of evolutionary linguistics within the generative/ biolinguistic perspective in attempts to explain the unparalleled human capacities for language. That said, the hypothesis proposed by the biolinguistic formalism have been challenged by empirical demonstrations which show divergence from the formal stipulations and demonstrate that there was never a single evolutionary event transforming pre-human into human species, but series of small and cumulative changes, resulting from multiple founder effects in the course of human evolution during Pleistocene. Moreover, human speciation is found to be a result of a combination of a number of evolutionary processes. A role for hybridization as a potential source of variation, complemented by genetic drift, found to have been major factors in the formation of the human phenotype, predominantly with neutral selective consequences, in interplay with selection offers a novel, integrative approach, presenting a significant challenge for the founder effect as a credible explanation for human evolution. Such challenge is extended to the bio-linguistic argument as explanation for the human capacity for language, i.e. both Language of Thought and Universal grammar. The argument for a founder effect in diversification of languages from a “ mother tongue”, based on assumed parallels between diversification of populations and languages, is challenged by demonstrations of divergence in migration patterns, population size and historical and cultural idiosyncrasies and favours an explanation for linguistic diversity resulting from self-organization.
