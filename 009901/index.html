The Acquisition of Pointing Signs and Pronominal Systems in Sign Languages
Diane Lillo-Martin, Deborah Chen Pichler
October 2025
 

In American Sign Language (ASL), pronominal functions are carried out by pointing signs: points to self for first-person, points to addressee or non-addressed persons or things as second- and third-person. This picture is complicated by the fact that pointing signs are also used in other ways in ASL: as one example, a signer can point to an abstract locus to refer to a non- present referent associated with that locus. Analysis of these pointing signs has been controversial, starting with arguments that points are not really linguistic elements, just deictics (Johnston 2013). Even when considered as linguistic, there are numerous debates over which analysis is the most appropriate. For example, Meier (1990) argues that the ASL pronominal system does not encode any grammatical distinction between 2nd and 3rd person, only a division between 1st and non-1st person. We provide evidence from the acquisition of ASL and from co-speech pointing in young hearing children to contribute to the discussion of how pointing signs should be analyzed. The acquisition pattern that emerges shows first that the distribution of points to objects versus people is very different. Both signing and non-signing children produce points to objects from an early age. However, only signing children develop the use of points to self and other people before 3;00, highlighting a difference between gestural and linguistic pointing. Furthermore, the development of points to different persons supports the linguistic analysis, and provides potential support for the 2nd/3rd-person distinction. On the other hand, points to abstract loci are not used by either group of young children, suggesting that this is yet another kind of element.
