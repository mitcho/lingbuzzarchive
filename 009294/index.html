How to Study Boundary Phenomena: English Reciprocals and the Limits of Categorization
Brett Reynolds
December 2025
 

English reciprocals (\textit{each other} and \textit{one another}) are generally classified as pronouns, but they are atypical and exhibit several characteristics of determinatives. As such, they exemplify a core problem in linguistic categorization: how to study boundary phenomena rigorously when the dataset consists of a small number of items that straddle categories. Rather than forcing a binary decision via selectively chosen diagnostics, I measure the stability of ambiguity. Using a comprehensive profile of 155 morphological, syntactic, semantic, and phonological properties, I locate the reciprocals in multidimensional grammatical space and apply five complementary lenses: distance measurement with permutation testing, a specification curve over metrics and feature blocks, comparison-group rotations, a simple mixture calibration, and calibration against known categories. Across these lenses, reciprocals consistently occupy a boundary position between canonical pronouns and compound determinatives, with mixture weights close to parity (approximately 0.53 and 0.49 for pronoun‑likeness) that remain stable across reasonable analytic choices. On a homeostatic property‑cluster view, this stability of ambiguity~-- together with cross‑dimensional tension (morphology pulling determinative‑ward, semantics pronoun‑ward)~-- is the expected signature of a genuine boundary. The framework generalizes as a template for transparent small‑$n$ categorization: it characterizes boundary behaviour without pretending to deliver decisive recategorizations. Within standard frameworks, reciprocals remain categorized as pronouns; the present results place them at the periphery, alongside morphologically complex items that realize fused grammatical functions.
