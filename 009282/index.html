Arguments Move at Least Once: Evidence from Split Constructions
Nikos Angelopoulos
September 2025
 

This paper proposes the Obligatory Movement Hypothesis (Kayne 2010)—the claim that all arguments undergo at least one instance of syntactic movement—by presenting novel evidence from discontinuous PP constructions (P-splits) in Modern Greek. While previous work has examined P-splits in intransitive contexts, I extend the analysis to transitive predicates, showing that the availability of P-splits is tightly constrained by the movement of direct objects. The data demonstrate that objects cannot remain in situ: their obligatory movement to a middle-field licensing position systematically blocks P-split formation, unless circumvented by cliticization or extraposition. This finding not only strengthens the empirical foundation for the Obligatory Movement Hypothesis but also has important consequences for the syntax of Greek. In particular, it challenges the standard in situ analysis of VSO orders, arguing instead that both subjects and objects occupy higher structural positions, with surface word order derived by remnant movement. More broadly, the results support a view of movement as a necessary component of argument licensing rather than an optional information-structural mechanism.
