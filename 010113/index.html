Fluency as Communicative Integration
Joseph Budinger
July 2026
 

Standard accounts of fluency face a classification problem. If fluency is defined by speech rate, pausing, repair behavior, automaticity, vocabulary coverage, native-like production, domain knowledge, or interactional ease, a native-speaking child who preserves a story-state despite missed words may be classified as less fluent than an adult second-language listener who recognizes more words but loses the event relation. This paper argues that such measures are local proxies, not the phenomenon itself. Fluency is communicative integration: the preservation of global event-coherence despite local incoherence. Coherence means the minimum event-state required for the next communicative frame to attach. The ordinary case is decisive: the same verbal form, What's up?, can function as a friendly greeting when said casually to a friend, a genuine request for information when said in response to visible concern, or an aggressive confrontation when said with hostile posture and tone. The words remain constant; only the surrounding frame cluster changes, and with it the communicative event. Across child comprehension, adult L2 listening, technical explanation, scripted production, nonverbal uptake, and damaged signal, fluency is shown by whether event-state survives and can be carried forward.
