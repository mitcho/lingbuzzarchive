A closer look at what/how we can learn from computational modelling of phonotactics
Karthik Durvasula
July 2026
 

Computational modelling has been extensively used in recent decades in attempts to understand and probe claims about the nature of phonotactic grammars. In this paper, I argue that much more care is needed in inferring aspects of the underlying competence system from model performance, even when compared against human acceptability judgements. I specifically focus on the issue of gradience and show using the Hayes & Wilson (Maximum-entropy based) Phonotactic Model that despite a good fit to acceptability judgments across three prior experimental results focussed on noncewords targetting a variation in word-initial consonant sequences, the model performance does not stem from gradience in the predictions, or gradience in constraints weights, or gradience in the training data (i.e., type frequency information). I further show that a minimal (categorical) phonotatic model does at least as well on all three experimental results. The latter model also reinforces the distinction between the idea of gradience due to the grammar (extensional gradience) and the grammar itself being gradient (intensional gradience). A more general point made is that, as with all theory testing using experiments, it is diﬀicult to learn about the underlying grammatical system from computational models if the models involve auxiliary hypotheses that themselves are not carefully explored and controlled for.
