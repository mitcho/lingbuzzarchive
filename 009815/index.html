Differentiation Before Frequency: Survival, Variation, and Internal Evaluation in Language
Amarendra Varma
March 2026
 

Languages routinely generate structurally valid variants, yet derivation alone does not guarantee their survival in use. This essay develops an account of how linguistic forms survive, spread, or are reshaped in use by examining a set of concrete cases, primarily from Telugu plural morphology but extending across registers, dialect contact, and cross-linguistic borrowing. Through a series of illustrations, I show that grammatically possible alternatives are not neutral once enacted in use. Instead, they diverge systematically according to how readily they integrate into ongoing articulatory and structural coordination. Building on this observation, I propose internal evaluation as a context-sensitive process of differentiation operating during speech enactment. By identifying this locus of differentiation, this study reframes variation, regional restriction, and linguistic change as outcomes of coordination-based differentiation during use, rather than as effects of frequency or conscious selection alone. In doing so, it clarifies a persistent explanatory gap in linguistic theory.
