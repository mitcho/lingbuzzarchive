Rocks, Rabbits, and Computers: Large Language Models Do Not Adjudicate (Relevant Forms of) Nativism and Empiricism
Vincent Carchidi
August 2026
 

The nativism and empiricism dispute in cognitive science turns on a disagreement about the domain-specificity or domain-generality of language’s biological basis. Biolinguists tend to adopt the former perspective, whereas competing approaches like usage-based approaches tend to adopt the latter. Where Large Language Models (LLMs) have been held up as theories or proxies for theories of human language acquisition or use, they have tended to be used in support of the view that these models provide an existence proof of natural language’s domain-general learnability. This paper reviews the dispute and argues that LLMs nevertheless fail to adjudicate nativism and empiricism. In short order, LLMs circumvent challenges relevant to language acquisition by human children in ways that reduce their significance to a theory of human language. These turn principally on an LLM’s relation to its environment during (pre- and post-) training, including the curation of domain-specific data on its behalf and its closure from extraneous environmental stimuli. It is argued that the hypothesis space that LLMs traverse during training is somewhat constrained by human engineers in virtue of this relation. In the same vein, it is argued that the Poverty of the Stimulus Argument to which LLMs are often aimed is not, in its form of most interest to linguists, an argument concerning the availability of data in the child’s environment, and the use of LLMs to this end miss the point.
