From Sound to Structure: How Human Language Traverses Fragility and Time
Chun-Hao Chan
July 2025
 

This paper is an extended sequel to Emotion-Driven Hypothesis for the Origins of Language (Chan, 2025), exploring why language—among all high-intelligence species—emerged only in humans and further evolved into a system capable of encoding obligations, institutions, and abstract knowledge through written and symbolized form. We argue that language did not arise merely as a tool for real-time information exchange, but as a compensatory system shaped by the evolutionary pressure of extended fragility. Through a sequential development from vocal imitation, narrative modeling, symbolic stabilization to written encapsulation, language gradually became a semantic apparatus capable of simulating futures, triggering collective actions, and constructing fictive orders. Integrating four critical dimensions—physiology, cognition, sociality, and temporality—this study proposes a four-stage model of pressure-based linguistic emergence and a three-tier framework for re-symbolization. By comparing humans with other high-cognition species such as great apes, we argue that language emerged not due to greater abilities, but in response to deeper pressures. Chapter 5 introduces five archetypal fictive orders—myth, religion, money, law, and knowledge—as historical branches of the language-based simulation system. In this model, language is not merely a medium of expression, but the semantic host of obligation and institutional logic. Finally, this paper reflects on the phenomenon of cognitive debt brought by excessive symbolization and the rise of large-scale language models (LLMs), calling for a renewed critical understanding of language as a generative engine of fiction and collective coordination. This research may serve scholars and interdisciplinary researchers interested in language origins, cognitive evolution, narrative neuroscience, social institutions, and AI simulation.
