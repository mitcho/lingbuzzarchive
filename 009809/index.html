Differential Object Marking in Heritage Italo-Romance: A Microcontact Perspective
Sorgini Luana
March 2026
 

This dissertation investigates Differential Object Marking (DOM) in heritage Italo-Romance varieties spoken in Argentina, situating the phenomenon within a broader Romance and cross-linguistic perspective. DOM, whereby only a subset of direct objects receives overt morphological marking, has been widely studied in Romance languages, yet little is known about its behaviour in heritage contexts where the dominant language—Argentinian Spanish—also exhibits a robust DOM system. The study addresses four central questions: (i) whether DOM changes in heritage grammars; (ii) whether such changes are contact-induced or internally motivated; (iii) how microcontact within bilingual communities differs from macrocontact with the societal majority language; and (iv) which structural mechanisms enable DOM to emerge or expand. Adopting a Minimalist framework, the dissertation analyses DOM in terms of structural Case assignment via Agree and the projection of nominal structure (NP/DP/KP). It combines typological comparison, diachronic analysis, and original fieldwork conducted in Italy and Argentina on six Italo-Romance varieties (Eastern Abruzzese, Calabrian, Friulian, Piedmontese, Sicilian, and Venetan), representing non-DOM, reduced-DOM, and fully grammaticalised DOM systems. The findings demonstrate that heritage speakers do not simply transfer Argentinian Spanish DOM. Instead, heritage systems develop expanded or restructured patterns grounded in pre-existing grammatical resources of the homeland varieties. Detailed analysis of Friulian reveals a diachronic continuum from Conservative Friulian—where a-marking is restricted to topicalised Accusative Experiencers—to Innovative and Heritage Friulian, where DOM extends to Accusative Themes and in-situ contexts. This expansion reflects endogenous grammaticalisation processes: microcontact with Argentinian Spanish plays a pivotal role in accelerating language change, yet it is not the ultimate trigger for it. Crucially, heritage DOM remains sensitive to animacy and information structure and does not replicate the Argentinian Spanish system wholesale. The dissertation argues that DOM in Romance occupies a hybrid position between accusative and dative domains, as shown by syntactic diagnostics (passivisation, clitic resumption, participial agreement). Historically, DOM emerges from the interaction between case erosion and topicalisation, with prepositional markers developing from Latin locative sources. In heritage contexts, this diachronic pathway interacts with bilingual restructuring, producing innovative yet systematically constrained object-marking systems. Overall, the study contributes to theories of case, grammaticalisation, and contact-induced change by demonstrating that heritage grammars are shaped by the interplay of internal structural options and external contact pressures, rather than by simple transfer from the dominant language.
