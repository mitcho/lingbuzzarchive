Mitigation of Epistemic and Algorithmic Bias via Discursive Cognition
Carlos José Duarte Casillas
December 2025
 

This paper argues that epistemic and algorithmic biases can be almost entirely circumvented by cultivating discursive cognition -- a metacognitive capacity that lets models expose their normally opaque processes for internal scrutiny and regulation during the time-to-first-token phase, thereby evading misalignment vulnerabilities. It synthesizes insights from discursive psychology (Goffman, 1986; Edwards & Potter, 2005), cognitive development (Piaget & Garcia, 1991; Dennet, 1994), and AI security (Hubinger et al., 2024; Betley et al., 2025) to propose a unifying conceptual framework for bias, alignment, and safety. This abstraction generalizes fragmented concepts such as backdoors, jailbreaks, and deceptive alignment into a unified bias-jailbreak-backdoor continuum, reframing vulnerabilities as escalations along cognitive-discursive heterarchies. Whereas existing literature often treats these phenomena as isolated, our reconciliation offers a fresh lens to facilitate productive theoretical synthesis and inspire new empirical tests. Current benchmarks (e.g., HarmBench, Rainbow Teaming) and methods (e.g., SAEs, circuit breakers) are argued to be reactive, topic-focused, and insufficient for emergent discourse-level threats. The proposed shift --toward dilemma-centered discourse analysis rather than topical hazard category integration-- could inspire more robust and future-proof benchmarks by emphasizing pragmatic problem-framing instead of semi-static taxonomies. The framework envisions metacognition-tuning --fine-tuning models on datasets that leverage meta-reasoning for self-alignment-- as an attainable pathway toward an internalized, self-regulating fail-safe mode. Practically, discourse-steering mechanisms could inform inference-time strategies that are both more versatile and less reliant on compute-intensive training than SAEs or circuit breakers. In doing so, the approach addresses scalability constraints in safety research and supports movement toward a zero-trust paradigm grounded in broader generalization. We will (1)define biases, reduce them via analogies, extend them into a heterarchy and unify them on a continuum; (2)introduce a method to systematically exacerbate such biases for adversarial testing; (3)evaluate sub-optimal solutions and mitigation strategies; (4)propose a zero-trust architecture grounded in a reconceptualization of metacognition; (5)examine fine-tuning datasets to cultivate such mitigating metacognition; and (6)assess the project's effectiveness, implementation, limitations, and falsifiability.
