On the Fundamental Difference between Dependent-up and Dependent-down Cases, and on their Manifestation in Turkic
Sabine Laszakovits
August 2025
 

Dependent cases in Dependent Case Theory (DCT) fall into two directions: during case competition between two nominals, “dependent-down” cases are assigned to the lower nominal (such as accusative), and “dependent-up” cases are assigned to the higher nominal (such as ergative). It is a well-known, not often discussed, issue why accusative case can be assigned to multiple arguments, while ergative case can only ever appear on one argument, and must appear on the highest argument. In this thesis, I argue that this is not accidental but follows from computational considerations: a formal grammar checking whether a given dependent-down case is licensed is less complex (in a formal sense) than a formal grammar checking whether a given dependent-up case is licensed: For a dependent-down case, the possible licensors are the NP’s c-commanders, which is a single sequence, while for a dependent-up case, the possible licensors (the NP’s c-commandees) need to be looked for in every local subtree. Chapter 2 gives an overview over the range of “versions” of DCT in the existing literature, and presents the assumptions made in this thesis. Chapter 3 reviews data from Turkic that illustrate that the same NP can serve as licensor for accusative on multiple lower arguments. Chapter 4 presents the argument that there is a complexity difference between dependent-up and dependent-down cases. Chapters 5 and 6 argue for a novel view on Turkic cases. Chapter 5 argues that in Turkic, dative is never a dependent-up case (against Baker & Vinokurova 2010), but always lexical/inherent: We don’t have evidence that it should be a dependent-up case, and (assuming chapter 4 is on the right track) because there is no Single Dative Generalization in Turkish. Chapter 6 argues that Turkic genitive is the dependent-up case in the nominal domain, assigned by case-competition against the possessum (Baker 2015a): If it were an unmarked case, we should observe GEN getting overwritten by movement into a different case assignment domain (like nominative), and its being an inherent/lexical case would violate Bobaljik’s (2008) Revised Moravcsik Hierarchy for the accessibility of cases for ϕ-agreement.
