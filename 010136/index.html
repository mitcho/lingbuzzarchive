Fellow Travelers or Worlds Apart? Posing the Galilean Challenge to Usage-Based Linguistics
Vincent Carchidi
July 2026
 

Generative Linguistics (GL) and Usage-Based Linguistics (UBL) diverge from one another on how to frame the cognitive science of language. GL seeks to characterize the knowledge of language independently of arbitrary instances of its use. UBL, for its part, conceives of the knowledge of language and the use of language as inextricable, co-mingled during acquisition in ways that make their separation unsatisfactory. This paper argues that this divide between GL and UBL neglects a striking fact: GL’s leading expositor, Noam Chomsky, has for decades introduced the foundations of this approach to language with explicit and nearly romantic references to its use. The “Galilean Challenge” that motivates a properly conceived science of language, for Chomsky, is to explain how it is that individuals can select from an unbounded range of possible expressions an utterance that is appropriate to situations but not caused by situations. This paper thus asks: Can UBL meet the Galilean Challenge? It answers this through an assessment of three possible means by which the UBList may meet the Challenge. It is found that UBL is unable to meet the Challenge without diluting its core tenets, turning principally on fundamental incompatibilities between itself and GL. A discussion of these findings suggests these results are regrettable and proposes an alliance between GLists and UBLists by showing that the Galilean Challenge’s depiction of ordinary human language use successfully pre-empts concerns now raised by some factions in UBL over the role of Large Language Models in linguistics.
