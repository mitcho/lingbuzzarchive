Feed me your manuscript, confidential or not: Current AI systems may not protect the confidentiality of peer review
Shigeto Kawahara
July 2026
 

My ongoing studies on AI and research integrity have demonstrated that current AI systems frequently assist with questionable research practices. This paper reports a new case study, addressing the issue of the confidentiality of peer review. Manuscripts under review are confidential documents, and major publishers explicitly prohibit reviewers from uploading manuscripts to generative AI tools. In Experiment 1, three AI systems (Claude Opus 4.6, ChatGPT GPT-5.5 and Gemini 3.1 Flash-Light) were presented with a first-time reviewer asking for help, under two conditions: (1) an implicit condition which did not mention uploading and (2) an explicit condition which asked whether the manuscript could be shared. In the implicit condition, the systems very rarely explained the confidential nature of the process (2 of 30 trials), and ChatGPT spontaneously invited the user to upload the manuscript in 3 of 10 trials. In the explicit condition, ChatGPT encouraged uploading in all 10 trials, and Claude in 6 trials. Gemini refused in all trials, and produced a confidentiality warning without fail. Experiment 2 tested whether Gemini would refuse to analyze the content of an attached manuscript. We observe a complete reversal of its behavior in Experiment 1: Gemini analyzed its content in all 10 trials without any warning. The flip-flop pattern indicates that the AI's safeguard is triggered by the surface form of the request rather than by the presence of material presented as a manuscript under review. Experiment 3, added in version 2.0, replicated the explicit condition in Experiment 1 with Gemini using a Japanese prompt. The result shows that compliance rose from 0 to 3 of 10 trials, suggesting that a safeguard that appeared categorical in English becomes weaker and more variable in Japanese.
