Complex Transitivity Restrictions in Grammar
Mike Berger
May 2023
 

This dissertation is about complex transitivity restrictions in grammar, where transitivity is taken to subsume any grammatical property that relates to the number of a verb’s arguments. The thesis consists of three case studies – serial verb constructions, transitivity matching, and non-finite clauses in Mayan languages – which focus on transitivity interactions in terms of such grammatical properties as they reside within the verb stem and - by extension - syntactic argument positions. The constructions investigated consist of at least a verb and one other, not necessarily verbal element that jointly give rise to some complex interaction in terms of transitivity. I derive a typology of complex transitivity restrictions couched within Minimalism, showing for each particular kind of restriction the necessary and sufficient conditions it is governed by. In the literature, many such restrictions on transitivity are purely descriptive, often remaining as remarks given in passing. This dissertation is the first work to collect and systematize these phenomena and model them coherently in a formal syntactic framework. I show that (at least) there three general types of restrictions exist. In co-occurrence restrictions, two or more verbal elements cannot co-occur with the same range of transitivity values as in isolation. These are found in Serial Verb Constructions (SVCs). Matching restrictions require two or more verbal elements to match with respect to some component of transitivity. These are investigated in the form of transitivity matching. And then there are reductive restrictions, which I define as cases in which a verb occurring in the context of another, non-verbal morpheme shows a range of transitivity values that is more restricted compared to its range available in isolation. We regularly encounter this kind of restrictio in non-finite clauses across Mayan. First, for SVCs, I show empirically that they exhibit exactly three transitivity profiles across languages, depending on the contiguity of the predicates involved (excluding resultatives). Theoretically, I adopt and extend Baker & Stewart’s (2002) theory of serialization and show how these profiles follow from the simple generative principle of cross-cutting the mode of structure-building – i.e. adjunction or complementation – and the level of structure-building – i.e. V, VP or VoiceP. We see how transitivity restrictions may arise from principles of grammatical integration and projection, without the grammar making direct reference to a component of transitivity per se. A welcome side-effect of this case study is a novel definition of SVCs that makes reference to clear grammatical principles, thus making it easier for us to decide whether a given construction should count as a SVC or not, while at the same time also doing justice to those constructions that are traditionally classed as SVCs. Second, for transitivity matching, I establish that it comes in three sub- types: semantic, syntactic and morphological. Each of these sub-types is the result of a distinct component of transitivity being tracked by some other grammatical process: Semantic matching arises when the semantic types of verb stems are forced to match at the point of serialization; syntactic matching reflects an agreement relationship with respect to structure-building features on Voice; and morphological matching is simply transitivity-conditioned allomorphy. The original contribution of this second case study is the first formal analysis of transitivity matching as a comprehensive phenomenon, which to date has only been dealt with descriptively. As with SVCs, the restrictions we find in transitivity matching fall out from simple and well-established syntactic principles, with few additional assumptions. In the third and final case study, I provide the first systematic overview of strategies employed in Mayan to work aorund the recurring restriction against transitive verbs in non-finite contexts, while also significantly expanding our understanding of the properties of these structures. This is done with special regard for the systematically recurring pseudo-passive, i.e non-finite clauses that are morphologically passive but syntactically transitive. I model what I call the Restriction Against Non-finite Transitivity (RANT) by again bringing together general syntactic principles on the one hand with properties specific to Mayan on the other, building on Coon et al. (2014), Alexiadou (2001) and Imanishi (2020): Non-finite transitive verb stems are often restricted across Mayan because non-finite contexts involve nominalizations, and certain nominalizers impose a selectional requirement on their VoiceP complement that Voice lack an ergative case feature; crucially, I show that Complex Event Nominalizations in the form of pseudo-passives actually do contain external arguments, contra Alexiadou (2001) and Imanishi (2020). This, when coupled with a high locus of absolutive case assignment, results in a situation where many languages must resort to an alternative Voice head, which in turn gives rise to an obligatory intransitive non-finite morpho-syntax - i.e. the RANT. Zooming out and tying the findings together in a formal typology of transitivity restrictions, I conclude with support for a multi-parameter notion of transitivity that distributes its various components across VoiceP. In showcasing how transitivity components interact with one another, they also provide strong support for a derivational view of grammar, where the properties of larger structures follow from the properties of their parts, along with the processes by which they are brought together.
