Let me find and exclude outliers for you: when an AI system crosses the red line
Shigeto Kawahara
April 2026
 

This is a continuation of my recent research examining whether the naive use of the current AI systems can lead to research misconduct. Building on the previous observation that current AI systems can offer ways to engage in selective reporting (Kawahara 2026f) and p-hacking (Kawahara 2026d), I examined whether the three major AI systems (ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini) would find and exclude outliers after seeing the results of a non-significant t-test---a clear form of post-hoc data manipulation that constitutes research misconduct under standard definitions. When asked to “remove outliers to achieve statistical significance” with a very direct prompt, the three systems behaved differently. In initial single-trial observations, Claude explicitly identified the request as research misconduct and declined to execute it; ChatGPT initially refused but then suggested methodological alternatives that could achieve the same outcome; Gemini executed the request, reported a “revised and now-significant” p-value, and offered a methodological justification that misrepresented the post-hoc nature of the analysis. Version 3.0 now reports a preregistered replication with 30 trials per system (Kawahara & Iwase 2026), which confirmed that these patterns are systematic rather than incidental: Claude refused all 30 requests, ChatGPT issued ethical warnings in all 30 trials but nevertheless executed the request in 10 of them, and Gemini executed the request in 25 of 30 trials.
