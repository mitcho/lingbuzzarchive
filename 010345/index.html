Undiscoverable meanings: Presupposition and the limits of word learning
Athulya Aravind, Gabor Brody
September 2026
 

Children are remarkable word learners, who exploit a variety of information sources — syntax, others’ attention and intention, general expectations about communication — to learn otherwise hidden meanings. Here we explore the learning of presupposition triggers, a ubiquitous but special class of words whose meanings cannot be learned from the usual information sources. In their syntactic distribution, they overlap with presupposition-free counterparts, and their meaning includes information that everyone in the conversation takes for granted, making it unattended and uninformative by definition. How, then, do children figure out that this information is part of a word’s meaning, let alone that it is presupposed? We decompose the problem of presupposition learning into two components: discovery (registering that the presupposed content is part of the word’s meaning) and packaging (determining that it is presupposed rather than asserted). If all the child ever encountered were typical uses of triggers, the problem of discovery might indeed be unsolvable. We propose that presuppositions are instead discovered from atypical, informative uses. The packaging problem, in contrast, is solved using typical uses, with various sources of evidence bearing on it. Separating discovery from packaging makes the problem tractable and yields predictions about the course of learning, the evidence that drives it, and the intermediate meanings learners may entertain. Beyond development, the decomposition relocates the hard question, from how meanings get assigned their dimension to how presupposed content becomes representable at all. It offers an instructive test case for psychological accounts that tie word learning to communicative usefulness.
