Havlík’s Law as a Syllabic Rule: A Structural Reanalysis of the Slavic Jers
Marko Jevremović
June 2026
 

Havlík’s Law is the classical description of jer behavior in Common Slavic, but its diachronic counting rule cannot be the synchronic rule modern Slavic speakers apply productively to new lexemes. This paper argues that Havlík’s Law is not a counting rule at all but the surface signature of a syllabification mechanism that operates at both temporal slices. A small ranked-constraint syllabifier decides which orthographic positions function as prosodic nuclei. Jers are not nuclei by default; they are promoted only when no other segment can serve. The central empirical contribution is two strictly sealed held-out tests totalling 60 OCS forms selected to lie outside every earlier corpus, with expected masks hand-labeled before each parser run, parameters frozen and md5-recorded, and the parser executed exactly once per sealed corpus. Combined result: 58/60; the two failures correspond to documented inventory gaps anticipated in ad- vance. The held-out test is supported by three further results: design corpus con- sistency on the Havlík corpus and the modern Serbian nepostojano A paradigmatic alternation (95 of 95 in-scope across three pre-design corpora); a non-stipulated structural prediction (a monosyllabic/polysyllabic split for word-initial sonorant forms recovering the minimal-word effect without an explicit constraint, corrobo- rated by Serbian rđa, rđav, rvati, mrknuti); and a cross-branch statistical signature on n = 29 + 30 across six Slavic branches showing categorically more divergence at jer-source positions than real-vowel positions (Mann-Whitney U = 44, z =−5.921, p < 10−6, rank-biserial effect = 0.899). A second, qualitatively different result complements this: single-parameter changes to the OCS-default parser reproduce daughter-branch surface forms (Serbian danas from disallowing the /dn-/ onset; Russian волк from penalizing syllabic /l/). Both are instances of one principle— when phonotactic pressure builds, the slot fills—and neither corresponds to any prediction the counting rule itself can make. The framework—empty-nucleus syllabification with ranked constraints—is not itself novel, aligning with Government Phonology (Kaye, 1990; Lowenstamm, 1996; Scheer, 2004, 2005) and classical generative phonology (Lightner, 1965; Gussmann, 1980; Rubach, 1984). What is novel is the unification: Havlík’s Law, modern Slavic nepostojano A, and a structurally tight sub-class of word-initial syllabic resonants reduce to a single mechanism, mechanized as an auditable algorithm and validated under sealed-test methodological discipline. The diachronic claim we defend is restricted: the jers were not vocalic at the time of the alternation pattern that became Havlík’s Law (approximately the 9th century onward); earlier phonetic content is treated as a separate question (Section 10.2.1).
