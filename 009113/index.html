Negation in Khuzestani Arabic and Sadat Tawaher Sign Language
Seyyed Hatam Tamimi Sa'd
December 2023
 

Human language is expressed mostly through either the aural-oral modality or the visual-gestural modality. In the aural-oral modality, humans encode information through speech via sound in the case of spoken languages while in the visual-gestural modality, information is encoded through gesture/sign as in the case of sign(ed) languages. Over time, attempts have been made to delineate the differences and similarities between spoken and sign languages on one hand, and between and across sign languages themselves on the other. These attempts fall within the field of cross-modal typology where attempts are made to propose a theory that would account for the differences and similarities found among various spoken and sign languages (Zeshan & Palfreyman, 2017). This dissertation brings spoken and sign language data together. I report on a young sign language called Sadat Tawaher Sign Language (STSL) that emerged naturally, in complete isolation from any other sign language / manual communication system, and without any external intervention such as deaf education. This incident transpired around sixty years ago in a small village named Sadat Tawaher in southwestern Iran after a man lost his hearing completely. Given that STSL emerged and matured within the vicinity of the spoken language Khuzestani Arabic (KhA), I attempted to answer the question whether STSL is a copied version of KhA. STSL users mostly consist of the family and close friends of the deaf individual who use this sign system only to communicate with him, but not among each other. Since STSL users speak KhA as their native language and verbally say what they sign, I hypothesized that STSL would logically conform in its grammatical structure to that of KhA. I narrowed down this question by examining negation in both KhA and STSL and gathered signed productions, story-telling data, and grammaticality judgments from five participants. Thus, the participants were bimodal bilinguals since they were fluent in a spoken and a signed language. Multiple manual signs were attested in the data which we glossed as NEGbasic, NEGproh, NEGposs, NEGexist, and another category, Other. Except for Other, each negator consists of two elements: a manual element and a non-manual element. These non-manual elements are as follows: backward head tilt for NEGbasic, side-to-side headshake for NEGproh and NEGexist, and air blown out of mouth for NEGposs. STSL has a SVO basic word order but also allows SOV which seems to be a derived order. KhA has a rather flexible word order and, under certain circumstances, allows all six possible word orders. However, SVO is the most common word order in KhA. The findings showed negation is strictly sentence-final in STSL whereas it is preverbal in KhA (i.e., S-Neg-VO in KhA and SVO-Neg in STSL). The results showed that in STSL the manual negators are required while the non-manual negators are optional. Thus, the manual negators may optionally be accompanied by non-manual negators. However, the non-manual markers were very frequently left out and the sentences were negated by use of manual negators only. Moreover, when present, the non-manual negator accompanies the manual negator only and never spreads on adjacent signs, constituents (e.g., VP) or the whole clause. Furthermore, in STSL the manual negator can negate the sentence on its own whereas the non-manual negator cannot do so. Thus, given these two facts, we classify STSL as typologically manual dominant which patterns with Italian Sign Language (LIS) and Turkish Sign Language (TİD) (Zeshan, 2006c). In analyzing the relationship between the manual and non-manual negators in STSL, we argue that the non-manual negator is part of the lexical description of the manual negator. Thus, since the non-manual marker constitutes part of the phonological make-up of the negative manual sign, in the absence of the manual negator, the non-manual negator cannot negate the sentence on its own as it cannot be phonologically realized. Therefore, the manual negator, being obligatory, along with the manual negator which may or may not be phonologically realized, occupies the head position of NegP. Spec,NegP, on the other hand, is occupied by a covert negative operator. Given that in STSL the manual negator cannot be absent and the non-manual negator never spreads onto the VP or whole clause, we argue that there is not a c-command relation. Instead, a spec-head relation is established between the manual negator and the covert negative operator. Furthermore, the findings clearly showed that more than one manual negator may be used in STSL without changing the polarity of the sentence; i.e., the sentence remains negative. This indicates that STSL is a negative concord (NC) language. However, the use of two negative particles in the same clause results in an affirmative reading in KhA, indicating that KhA is a double negation (DN) language. In STSL, NC serves an emphatic purpose only. As a result, the first manual negator in NC constructions serves sentential negation while the other manual negator(s) are negative adverbials. Therefore, STSL conforms to a different word order in its negative constructions from that of KhA. Furthermore, while producing negative sentences in speech and sign, the participants simultaneously adhered to the KhA grammar and STSL grammar, producing S-Neg-VO and SVO-Neg orders. This finding confirms that the human brain is capable of the simultaneous production of two different grammars when physical constraints are removed, that is, when language users can encode information through two different channels, e.g., auditory-oral modality and visual-gestural modality. In conclusion, STSL has its own grammar independent of KhA likely due to modality effects of the channel through which it communicates information.
