Noticing language: What echolocation tells us about language emergence
Brett Reynolds
January 2026
 

Why do humans alone develop language? Rather than specialized processing modules, I argue that evolution selected for a simple attentional bias, hyperpriors that amplify communicative signals (eye contact, infant-directed prosody, hierarchical rhythm). This precision-weighting ensures linguistic patterns dominate learning without requiring language-specific architecture. Echolocation demonstrates the principle. Blind humans achieve remarkable spatial discrimination through self-generated sounds, a complex capacity that emerges without specialized adaptations. This spandrel falsifies the common inference that complex abilities require direct selection. If echolocation can arise from generic neural systems plus experience, so can language, given appropriate attentional focus. The attention hypothesis explains why language emerges universally while echolocation remains rare (only language has inherited attentional support), why other vocal learners never develop language (they lack the relevant hyperpriors), and why adults struggle with low-salience L2 features (they lack children's automatic orientation to these patterns). Three experiments could test this: cross-species neonatal preference tests, computational models varying only attention parameters, and fNIRS measurement of precision-weighting in newborns. The account reframes language evolution as selection for what we notice, not what we compute.
