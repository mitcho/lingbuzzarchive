Why clause structure is judged like tense and agreement: A coordination account of grammaticality
Brett Reynolds
January 2026
 

Grammaticality judgments are selective: speakers treat violations of agreement, clause structure, tense, or evidential marking as straightforward errors of "grammar", while treating accent shifts, many lexical substitutions, and register choices as matters of style or appropriateness. Why? This paper argues that the difference tracks what kind of work a linguistic contrast does. Some contrasts serve as shared infrastructure for public coordination: they configure what kind of move an utterance counts as, who is committed to what, and what responses are licensed. These contrasts (clause typing, argument linking, polarity, tense--aspect where grammaticalized) form closed paradigms whose values have to be mutually known for coordination to succeed. I call them "operators". Other contrasts, such as accent, open-class vocabulary, and register markers, primarily contribute indexical or conceptual content that remains negotiable; a mismatch invites clarification or social judgment, not structural rejection. Clausal architecture belongs with tense and agreement because it packages operators, not because it's autonomous from meaning. From an information-theoretic perspective, operators function like protocol headers: they carry few bits themselves but cause large entropy reduction downstream, which explains why a wrong value can crash coordination even when the utterance is otherwise intelligible. The proposal predicts that categorical judgments, distinct processing signatures, and strong preemption effects should cluster around operator contrasts cross-linguistically, including tonal or gestural exponents where these are grammaticalized as operators.
