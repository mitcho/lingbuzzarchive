Monomorphemic verbs in Mandarin Chinese and their anticausative vs. antiagentive alternants
Fabienne Martin, Hongyuan Sun, Jinhong Liu, Hamida Demirdache
July 2026
 

Current title: Zero-change construals as a probe into event structure: Mandarin Chinese simple verbs and their anticausative vs. antiagentive alternants. Speakers of many languages can say the equivalent of `I burned the book, but it didn’t burn at all', asserting an action while denying that the change it aims at ever began. We refer to this interpretation as 'zero-change'. We show that variation in its availability across syntactic frames in Mandarin simple verbs provides a novel probe into event structure. We start from the proposal that the relation between a verbal event and its result state is not fixed in the lexicon (Martin et al. 2025). This allows one and the same verb to describe either a full causation event when its subject is an agent, or a mere change when the cause is either demoted or externalized as a causer subject. We establish that zero-change construals survive in some intransitive frames but not others, thereby teasing apart two agent-demoting operations commonly conflated: the familiar causative/ anticausative alternation and what we call the agentive/ antiagentive alternation. Distinct from passives and middles, it is attested well beyond Mandarin. Zero-change construals in Mandarin arise from two distinct sources: degree semantics (whether verbs encode a degree of event realization, rather than a degree of change), and aspect (whether languages have a partitive, non-completive perfective). Taken together, these findings yield a three-way typology of Mandarin simple verbs—change-of-state, target-state-oriented activity, and pure activity—and contribute to a broader typology of how, and under which conditions, languages allow non-culminating accomplishments. This typology tracks Cheng’s (1989) generalization linking telicity to participation in the transitive/intransitive alternation. We explain this correlation by showing that both properties depend on whether the event provides evidence for a state property of the theme. The three verb classes differ in how this state property is determined: it is lexically encoded in change-of-state verbs, conventionally associated with target-state-oriented activities, and, in marked cases, supplied by contextual enrichment with pure activities.
