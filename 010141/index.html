Probability in Closed Symbolic Systems: When Classical Probability Proves What Statistics Cannot
Aya Katz
July 2026
 

This paper develops a framework for using classical probability, rather than frequentist statistics, to assess relatedness and origins in closed symbolic systems: domains characterized by a small finite alphabet, well-defined combinatorial rules, and effective closure against external input. Frequentist statistics is designed for inference from repeated sampling of an open or incompletely specified population; classical probability is designed for reasoning about a single case within a fully specified, finite universe of possibilities. When a domain meets the criteria for closure, classical probability provides a more direct, transparent, and philosophically appropriate tool than statistical significance testing — particularly for singular, unrepeatable events for which no sample can be drawn. The framework is illustrated with two extended case studies: the identity between the Proto-Indo-European copula root and third-person pronoun root, and the extreme conservation of ultraconserved non-coding elements in vertebrate genomes. Further applications — to Hebrew computational linguistics, language acquisition, and the evaluation of singular communicative events such as a pet parrot's unprompted, contextually accurate alarm call — extend the framework's reach across historical linguistics, comparative genomics, and cognitive science.
