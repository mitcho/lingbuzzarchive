Theoretical Approaches to Comparative Syntax
Sjef Barbiers, Guido Vanden Wyngaerd, Jenneke van der Wal
October 2025
 

Human languages vary in the ways phrases and clauses can be constructed from their building blocks, i.e. their grammatical features, morphemes, and words. The goal of comparative syntax is to discover not only the areas where languages differ, but also where they are the same, i.e. to discover the building blocks and principles that all human languages have in common. Accordingly, a theoretical model of the human language faculty should explain both the range and limits of syntactic variation, as they can be found both across and within languages. The aim of this chapter is to review a selection of closely related generative approaches to this question, with a particular focus on two broad theories of variation. The first is one that restricts the source of all syntactic variation to the lexicon, an approach that goes by the name of the Borer-Chomsky Conjecture (BCC; see below for more discussion). The second theory takes the concept of a parameter to be central, and we accordingly devote special attention to the theory of parameters, including the more recent attempts to reconcile the BCC with parameter theory.
