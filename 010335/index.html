What Frequency Matching Might (not) Be
Caleb Belth
September 2026
 

Frequency matching has been proposed as a central principle underlying phonological knowledge and learning. A central example comes from Hungarian vowel harmony, where stems with back vowels followed by neutral vowel(s) show structurally-conditioned lexical variation in whether they take back or front suffixes. In wug tests, Hungarian speakers show evidence of internalizing this structural conditioning, often taken as evidence of speakers internalizing a probabilistic grammar. This article critically analyzes the Frequency Matching Hypothesis and argues that the appearance of frequency matching emerges from variation across speaker vocabularies and from averaging over categories that may not reflect those internalized by speakers. I suggest that the central question of lexical variation is how learners discover its structural conditioning and propose a learning theory, in the form of an independently-motivated learning model, for that question. Assuming just variation across speaker vocabularies, the model exhibits the appearance of frequency matching. The model matches wug test results as well as previously proposed probabilistic grammars, predicts a diachronic shift in Hungarian vowel transparency, and explains a surprising lack of token-level variation in Hungarian vowel harmony.
