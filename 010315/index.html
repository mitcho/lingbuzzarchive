The Nanosyntax of the Adjective. Evidence from Czech
Pavel Caha, Karen De Clercq, Michal Starke, Guido Vanden Wyngaerd
September 2026
 

This book follows up on the seminal work by Bobaljik, who proposed a containment relation among the positive, comparative and superlative degrees. The starting point of our work was the observation that some languages provide a much richer morphology: we discovered cases where the comparative is derived from the positive by the addition of two or even three independent suffixes, while in other cases the comparative is not derived from the positive, but from a shorter, truncated form of the positive. We argue that the full morphological complexity can be derived by unifying the original claim that the superlative syntactically contains the comparative, and the comparative syntactically contains the positive with a more fine-grained decomposition, using the ingredients familiar from the semantics research distinguishing scales, standards of comparison, measure phrases, etc. Specifically, we argue for a syntactic representation consisting of two scales, with the comparative showing both scales and the positive only one, along the lines of Kennedy and Levin’s 2008 work. Each scale is in turn represented syntactically by the same four features of standard of comparison, dimension of the scale (e.g., size or speed), direction of the scale (positive vs. negative, as in fast vs. slow) and perhaps a measure phrase (which we call ‘distance’). We show that this combination yields exactly the rich morpho-syntactic patterns observed.
