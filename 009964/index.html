Excavating grammatical fossils: Effects and causes in the semantic organization of Swahili noun classes
Matt Malone, Jordan Kodner, Spencer Caplan
August 2026
 

Patterns in language data are standardly taken as evidence for the grammar that generates them, yet regularities in usage need not reflect any synchronic grammatical cause. The semantic basis for Bantu languages' noun class systems, debated for decades, is a case in point. Using distributional embeddings trained on the largest annotated corpus of Swahili, we find that noun classes are semantically ``compact'' above chance, suggesting at least partial organization by meaning. However, analysis using the Tolerance Principle, a theory of linguistic generalization, reveals that just one of 18 previously proposed semantic features, Human, is learnable as a productive rule. To explore the source of these unproductive patterns, we simulate earlier stages of Swahili through loanword elimination, finding noun classes were both more compact and closer to productivity. Together, these distributional, acquisition, and diachronic results indicate that the semantic organization of Swahili noun classes is no longer part of speakers' grammars. We argue it is a grammatical fossil: a pattern once actively imposed by the grammar, now persisting only as an eroding remnant. More broadly, we propose a framework for identifying grammatical fossils, distinguishing measurable patterns in language data from the grammatical mechanisms, or absence thereof, that generate them.
