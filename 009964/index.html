Excavating grammatical fossils: Effects and causes in the semantic organization of Swahili noun classes
Matt Malone, Jordan Kodner, Spencer Caplan
May 2026
 

The extent of a semantic basis of Bantu languages' noun class systems has been debated for decades, with proposals ranging from systematic feature-based assignment to total arbitrariness. Using distributional word embeddings trained on the largest annotated corpus of Swahili, we find that most noun classes are semantically ``compact'' above chance, suggesting at least partial organization by meaning. However, further analysis applying the Tolerance Principle, a theory of linguistic generalizations, finds just one of 18 previously proposed semantic features, Human, is learnable as a productive rule. To explore the source of these otherwise unproductive patterns, we simulate earlier stages of Swahili through loanword elimination, finding that noun classes were both more compact and closer to productivity in earlier eras. Together, these distributional, acquisition, and diachronic results indicate that the semantic organization of Swahili noun classes is no longer a synchronic part of speakers' grammars. We argue it is a grammatical fossil: a pattern once actively imposed by the grammar, now persisting only as an eroding remnant. More broadly, we propose a framework for identifying and interpreting grammatical fossils, drawing a crucial distinction between measurable patterns in language data and the grammatical mechanisms, or absence thereof, that generate them.
